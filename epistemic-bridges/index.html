Epistemic Bridges

Do language models reach the connections people make?

Sofia G. Gallego · CosmoVentures, Paris · Play at mapideas.org/bridges · Paper and code at github.com/nefinia/epistemic-bridges

In short

Creativity often happens in the space between ideas, where concepts that seem unrelated turn out to connect. In Bridges, a short web game, people link two simple ideas, such as a bee and a mirror, in any way they like. The same pairs went to 15 open language models, from GPT-2 (2019) to Qwen3 (2025). The players' answers draw a map of where people go, and each model is described by how much of that map it reaches, with no judge: every answer is placed in a space of meaning, and the rest is counting.

Measured the usual way, chat training seems to close the map almost completely. Most of that comes from the voice: chat models wrap every answer in the same way of speaking. With the voice removed from every answer in the same way, a smaller gap remains in the ideas, clearest for the newest models.

The game and the map

Two ideas appear, joined by an arrow, and the player writes how to get from one to the other: a sentence, a chain of words, a memory. Six pairs go to everyone, two concrete, two mixed and two abstract, each in one direction. Twenty-two players wrote 115 bridges, and the main comparison uses the 86 English answers from the 16 players who did all six. The models answered the same text ten times per pair and direction, with no persona, with reasoning personas, with creative conditions and at different temperatures.

Each answer becomes a point in a space of meaning, where answers that say similar things sit close together. Around each player's answer there is a small sphere, and together these spheres are the human map. For a model, two numbers follow: how many of its answers land on the map, and how many of the places people went it comes close to. People cannot be measured against a map they drew, so the players are split in two halves many times, and one half is placed on the other half's map like a model.

The voice and the ideas

Measured the usual way, the results look dramatic. The same Qwen2.5 7B model reaches about half of the places people went before chat training, and 1 in 17 after. The newest model, Qwen3 8B, reaches 1 in 100. Looking at the answers explains much of it. A typical chat answer begins: “While the transition from a bee to a mirror may seem inexplicably bizarre on the surface, we can creatively construct a connection through a series of steps…”. Almost every chat answer opens like this, so their first sentences all land on the same spot.

That shared voice can be measured on its own, as how often a set of answers repeats the same sentence patterns. Chat models repeat more than people, up to a third more for the Qwen models from 7 to 14 billion parameters. Base models, trained only to continue text, repeat a little less than people. Chat training gives the models one voice.

The ideas can be measured on their own too. Every answer, from players and models alike, loses the sentences that only talk about the task, and keeps its content words. On this map of ideas, half of the players reach 8 of every 10 places the other half went, and so do the base models and most older and smaller chat models. The newest and largest reach about 6: Qwen3 8B 62%, Qwen3 32B 57%. They also take the most common connection in about half of their answers, against one in three for people. gpt-oss 20B stays close to people in ideas, and still takes the common connection more often than any model.

Personas and randomness

Asked to answer with a persona, chat models change their voice more than their ideas. Every creative condition makes the sentence patterns more varied. In ideas, most stay within the noise, and a few narrow the map: with a story, Qwen2.5 7B reaches only about a third of it, as if the story became the common connection. One condition widens the ideas on every model: answering as an ordinary person drawn at random, such as a 62-year-old fisherwoman in La Serena who mends her own nets. It adds about one place in ten. The reasoning personas from earlier work on false epistemic redundancy change little, and the one that explains everything through purpose narrows the map by about four places in ten.

Randomness reaches further and loses its way. At a temperature of 1.6, Qwen2.5 7B reaches more of the map of ideas than with any persona, while a third of its answers land where no person went; at 2.0, more than half. The ordinary person moves the answers toward places people go.

Pairs and direction

Abstract pairs lowered people's variety only a little. A strong shared connection lowers it more: for spider and melody, more than half of the players went through a web or a nursery rhyme. For chat models, the direction of the arrow changes the answer in 35 of 60 cases; for people, in none of the six pairs, although with so few answers per direction a small effect would be hard to see.

The warm-up, before and after ChatGPT

The ten-word warm-up links today's players to people tested before AI chatbots. Players today score 83 on average, against 78 in the published scores from before ChatGPT, within the same range. There is no sign here that people have become less divergent, although the two groups of people differ. The models' warm-up scores do not follow their bridges: Qwen3 scores 79 to 80 on single words and reaches the least of the map of ideas.

What no model reaches

Some bridges stay far from every AI answer, even with every persona and temperature together. The farthest are personal: a wish kept secret, a place from childhood, an object reused in a garden, an afternoon at the sea. After them come an old meaning of a word, and play with the sound or the letters of the words themselves.

What it suggests

Chat training changes the voice first and the ideas less. The voice matters on its own: a connection said in a person's own words carries something of that person, and when every answer arrives in the same voice, readers meet one way of thinking. Under the voice, the newest models still reach less of where people go and return more often to the common connection. Measuring diversity in AI needs the two apart, since the usual measure mixed them and changed tenfold once they were separated.

A harmonic interaction between people and machines would preserve what people do well, the personal, the sensory, the playful, each in its own voice, and add what machines do well, such as speed and breadth across disciplines. The ordinary person drawn at random points to one simple way: many lives, many voices, and a measure of how much ground they cover together.

How sure these numbers are

Repeating the measurement eleven times moves the places reached by 1 to 3 in a hundred. A second batch of answers from the same model moves them by 3 to 7. Leaving out one player at a time moves them by 5 to 12, so with 16 players only the larger gaps are clear: the voice of chat models, and the gap between people and the newest models in ideas. The effect of chat training on the ideas of one 7B model is within that uncertainty. More players, from other fields and languages, are the next step.
