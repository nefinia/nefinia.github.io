Epistemic Bridges

Do language models reach the connections people make?

Sofia G. Gallego · CosmoVentures, Paris · Play at mapideas.org/bridges · Paper and code at github.com/nefinia/epistemic-bridges

In short

Creativity often happens in the space between ideas, where concepts that seem unrelated turn out to connect. In the web game Bridges, players link two simple ideas, such as a bee and a mirror, in any form. The same pairs went to 15 open models from 2019 to 2025. The human answers draw a map of where people go. Each model is described, without any judge, by how many of its answers land on that map and how much of the map it reaches.

Measured as written, chat training seems to close the map. Most of that gap is the voice. With the voice removed from every answer in the same way, the newest chat models still reach less of the ideas than people, and take the most common connection more often.

The game

Each round shows two ideas joined by a dashed arrow, and the player writes how to get from one to the other: a sentence, a chain of words, a memory. Six pairs go to every player, two concrete, two mixed and two abstract, each in one direction. Twenty-two players wrote 115 bridges, and the main comparison uses the 86 English answers of the 16 players who did all six. A typical answer has 9 words.

The same pairs, in the same words, went to the models, ten times per pair and direction. A base model is trained only to continue text. Chat models go through a second stage of training that teaches them to answer as a helpful assistant, with the clear, safe and sensible answer that people rate highly. People cannot be scored against a map they drew, so the players are split in two halves, and one half is placed on the other half's map like a model.

Voice or ideas

As written, Qwen2.5 7B reaches 54% of the human map before chat training and 6% after, and the newest model, Qwen3 8B, reaches 1%. A look at the answers changes the picture. A typical chat answer begins: “While the transition from a bee to a mirror may seem inexplicably bizarre on the surface, we can creatively construct a connection through a series of steps…”. Most chat answers open this way, so they all land on the same spot.

Chat training gives the models a voice, and the voice is shared: their sentence patterns repeat more than people's, most of all for the Qwen models from 7 to 14 billion parameters, while base models repeat less than people.

With the voice removed from every answer in the same way, the gap in ideas is much smaller. Half of the players reach 83% of the other half's map of ideas, and 83% of their answers land on it. The base models reach as far, while a third or more of their answers fall where no person went: they reach by scattering. The older and smaller chat models reach about as far too. The newer and larger ones reach less: Qwen2.5 7B 68%, Qwen3 8B 62%, Qwen3 32B 57%. They also take the most common connection, like the hourglass for salt and clock, in about half of their answers, against a third for people.

Personas and randomness

As written, every creative condition seemed to open the map. Measured apart, they mostly change the voice. Only an ordinary life drawn at random, such as a fisherwoman in La Serena who mends her own nets, widens the ideas on every model. With a story, the newest models narrow the ideas, as if the story itself became the common connection. The reasoning personas from false epistemic redundancy change the ideas little, or narrow them.

Randomness gives a different shape. More temperature reaches more of the map of ideas, and more of the answers fall where no person went. Randomness spreads the answers; an ordinary life moves them toward places people go.

Pairs and direction

Abstract pairs change people's answers less than expected. What lowers diversity is a strong shared connection, like the web or the nursery rhyme for spider and melody. For chat models the starting idea changes the answer in 35 of 60 cases; for people, in none of the six pairs, although with so few answers a small effect would be hard to see.

The warm-up, before and after ChatGPT

The ten-word warm-up links today's players to people tested before AI chatbots. Players today score 83, against 78 in the published scores from before ChatGPT. There is no sign that people have become less divergent, although the two samples differ. The models' scores do not follow their bridges: Qwen3 scores 80 on single words and reaches the least.

What no model reaches

Some human bridges stay far from all AI answers. The farthest are personal: a wish kept secret, a place from childhood, an object reused in a garden, an afternoon at the sea. After them come an old meaning of a word, and play with the sound of the words themselves.

What it suggests

Chat training narrows the voice first, and the ideas less. The voice matters in its own right: a connection said in a person's own words carries something of that person, and when every answer arrives in the same voice, readers meet one way of thinking. Measuring diversity needs the voice and the ideas apart.

A harmonic interaction would preserve what people do well, the personal, the sensory, the playful, each in its own voice, and enhance what machines can add, such as speed and breadth across disciplines.

Limits

The human map comes from 16 players, mostly engineers who use AI every day, so only the larger gaps are clear: the voice of chat models, and the gap between people and the newest models in ideas. More players, from other fields and languages, are the next step.
