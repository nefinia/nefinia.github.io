An initiative in formation

Peer review should work for scientists.

We’re exploring a scientist-owned cooperative for independent peer review, where researchers are compensated for their expertise and have a voice in how the system is run.

Join the founding network → See how it could work

An early-stage initiative currently bringing together interested researchers, editors, and institutions.

§ The problem

1A structural description, not a verdict on anyone who reviews today.

Essential scientific work, done on goodwill and spare time.1

Peer review is how the scientific community checks its own work. It relies on researchers reading manuscripts closely, testing claims, and writing careful reports. In most cases they are expected to do this without compensation, alongside their research, teaching and administrative duties.

As submission volumes grow, finding available reviewers takes longer, and manuscripts can wait months for an assessment. Meanwhile, the scientists who provide the intellectual labour have little ownership of the infrastructure through which peer review happens.

Uncompensated

Reviewing is generally treated as a professional duty rather than paid expert work.

Stretched

More submissions and limited reviewer time can lead to long delays for authors.

Not owned

Reviewers rarely have a say in how the systems that depend on them are governed.

§ The idea

2Two flows, one meeting point: the reviewer.

Evaluation as its own layer, funded as shared infrastructure.2

Research can already be shared through preprints and repositories. Independent peer review could then provide a trusted, portable layer of evaluation, supported by the institutions that rely on it, with the funding flowing to the researchers doing the reviewing.

§ How it could work

3A proposed model. The specific mechanisms will be developed with the scientific community.

From manuscript to a review record that travels with the work.3

  1. 1

    Submission

    A researcher submits a manuscript or an existing preprint for review.

  2. 2

    Matching

    Qualified researchers are identified based on relevant expertise, and conflicts of interest are checked before anyone is invited.

  3. 3

    Independent review

    Reviewers evaluate the work and are compensated for their time, whether their assessment is positive or negative.

  4. 4

    Response and revision

    Authors respond to the reports and revise the manuscript.

  5. 5

    Persistent review record

    The manuscript receives a record containing the review history, versions, author responses, and relevant verification information.

  6. 6

    Portable reviews with consent

    With the agreement of authors and reviewers, reviews could be public and portable, rather than disappearing when a manuscript moves somewhere else.

§ Why a cooperative

4Owned by the people whose expertise makes it work.

Scientists already provide the expertise that makes peer review possible. We think they should also have a voice in how its infrastructure is governed.4

In a cooperative, participating researchers are members, not just contributors. Members could help decide:

GovernanceWho decides what, how members vote, and how leadership is accountable.
StandardsWhat a rigorous, fair review should include across different fields.
Compensation principlesHow reviewers are paid, and how rates are set openly.
Transparency rulesWhat is published, what stays confidential, and who chooses.
Future developmentWhich tools, services and partnerships the network builds next.
Principle

Scientific judgment must remain independent from financial incentives. Reviewers are paid for their time and expertise, never for the outcome of their assessment, and funders do not influence individual reviews.

§ Longer-term vision

5A complement to existing publishing, not a replacement for it.

Dissemination and evaluation no longer need to be the same event.5

Preprints make research available immediately. Independent peer review could then operate as a separate layer of evaluation, with its results attached to the work wherever it goes.

We are exploring whether independent, portable and researcher-governed peer review could complement existing publishing systems and potentially support new models of scientific communication. Journals could draw on these reviews, just as authors and readers could.
